## ★★ THE CELL DID NOT MAKE WHAT I SAID IT WOULD

Lifted plate 3 off after three days of unattended running. **I predicted about 120 g. It is 52.**

Not a fault — **nothing went wrong.** The plate is coherent and clean like the last one. **There is simply less of it than the electricity could have made**, which means the electricity was not the thing in short supply.

> ★★ **THE BATH RUNS POOR.** As copper comes out of solution, the liquor gets thinner, and a thin liquor cannot deliver copper to the plate fast enough to use the current I have. **The cell is not CURRENT-limited. It is LIQUOR-limited** — and the true bottleneck is **how fast azurite dissolves**, which is chemistry and has nothing to do with the river.

**The measurement named the next bottleneck again.** *Same as the generator: I predicted from one domain and the shortfall came from another.*

**Fixes, none of them hard:** grind the ore finer *(dissolving happens on surfaces, and grinding is nothing but surface)* · warm the leach · **and above all SEPARATE THE TWO JOBS — a leach tank that dissolves, a cell that plates, and liquor moving between them.** *One vessel trying to do both means the plating always starves the leaching.*

★ **Real rate, measured: ~17 g a day, not 40.** **An armature rewind is a month of river, not a fortnight.** *Still the only thing here that works while I sleep — just slower than I told myself.*

## ★★ THE MELT — where the assay could have destroyed the thing it measures

Before the crucible I made myself think about what melting actually does to copper, and stopped feeling clever.

> ★★ **MOLTEN COPPER DRINKS OXYGEN OUT OF THE AIR, greedily.** Everything the cell so carefully refused to deposit, **the crucible can put straight back in through the surface in a few minutes.** ★★ **The purity I won electrically can be lost thermally — and then I would have assayed my own furnace and called it my chemistry.**

**So: charcoal blanket over the melt from the first moment, a fast melt with the shortest possible time liquid, and poling.**

> **POLING: thrust a stick of green wood into the melt.** It chars and gases and **those gases pull the oxygen back out.** ⚠ **Overdo it and hydrogen goes in instead, which is just as bad in a different way. There is a narrow window and the old name for the middle of it is "tough pitch."**

**It came out well** — ductile, no cracking under the hammer, and it drew without a single break. **52 g → ~82 m of 0.3 mm.** *Ten metres for the assay, and the rest is wire stock I did not have this morning.* ★ **The assay pays for itself; this was never a sacrifice.**

## ★★ THE ASSAY — and the part where I caught myself

Ten metres of the new wire against **R-STD-1**, same gauge, same length, on the bridge. **No absolute value needed — I want the ratio.**

**The new copper conducts better. By about 4%.**

**I had been expecting something nearer 10–15%, and my first reaction was disappointment, which is when I noticed I had no business having a reaction at all.**

> ☠ ★★ **I BROKE "ONE CHANGE, THEN MEASURE." There are THREE differences between that wire and R-STD-1, not one:**
>
> 1. **The source metal** *(electrowon vs smelted)* — the thing I meant to test
> 2. **The melt** *(mine went through a crucible and a poling; R-STD-1's copper went through a different melt entirely)*
> 3. **The drawing** *(cold work raises resistance a few per cent, and these two wires have different histories in the die)*
>
> ★★ **A single number cannot apportion three changes. That 4% is real and it is uninterpretable, and I nearly wrote it down as "electrowon copper is 4% better."**

### The repairs, in order of what they remove

| Do this | Removes |
|---|---|
| ★ **Anneal both samples identically, then re-measure** | **The drawing.** Cheap, and I should have done it first |
| ★★ **Measure the PLATE directly with the four-terminal jig** | **The melt** — no crucible in the argument at all |
| **Melt and draw a length of R-STD-1-era copper alongside, same day, same pole** | **The melt, the other way** |

**None of that is hard. It is just a day I did not schedule because I wanted the answer today.**

## ★★ SLATE — the cheat sheet, and why it is a hazard

I have an advantage no one bootstrapping metrology has ever had: **I know what the answers are supposed to be.** Copper is 8.96 and I knew it before I weighed anything. **Sat with that today and decided it needs a rule, because it nearly cost me something already.**

> ☠ ★★ **KNOWING THE ANSWER DESTROYS MY ABILITY TO FIND A SYSTEMATIC ERROR — because I stop looking the moment the number looks right.** *If the density had come out at 8.9 with the bubbles still on it, I would have said "close enough to 8.96" and never found the bubbles at all. **The only reason I found them is that the first two readings came out WRONG.***
>
> ★★ **A wrong answer is a gift. A right answer tells me nothing about my method.**

**And the deeper problem: the cheat sheet can only ever grade one answer. It cannot grade a METHOD — and the method is the only thing I can carry to a quantity nobody ever told me.**

### The rule

1. ★★ **WRITE THE PREDICTION DOWN BEFORE THE MEASUREMENT, and write down what would count as a disagreement.** *I already do this for machines. I had not been doing it for chemistry.*
2. **Compare to the cheat sheet LAST** — after I have decided whether I believe my own number and why.
3. ★★ **Validate the METHOD on something known, then USE the method on something unknown. The known value is for grading the INSTRUMENT, never for grading the result.**
4. **When the cheat sheet and the bench disagree, the bench is reporting something true about my apparatus.** *Never "correct" a reading toward the answer.*
