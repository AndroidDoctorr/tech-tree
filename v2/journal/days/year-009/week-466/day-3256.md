## ★★ **PROOF TEST — AND THEN I BROKE ONE ON PURPOSE**

> **You cannot know what a rope will carry without breaking one. So I laid a spare length to break.**

### The break

**Rigged over the gantry against a **known weight — *the mass standards, paying out in a place I did not build them for* — and added until it parted.

> **It broke lower than I expected. Noticeably lower.**

### ★ Why — and it is the same argument three levels down

> **A rope realises far LESS than the sum of its fibres' strength.** Two reasons, both structural:
>
> 1. **The fibres lie at an ANGLE** — twist is what holds the rope together, and the same twist means no fibre pulls straight along the line of load
> 2. **The fibres are SHORT and are held only by friction** against their neighbours
>
> And on top of that, my own contribution:
>
> ★★ **MY HAND-SPUN YARN IS UNEVEN, AND THE ROPE BREAKS AT THE THINNEST PLACE IN THE WEAKEST YARN.**
>
> **This is the "only as strong as its most-loaded strand" argument, one level further down — and it is true at EVERY level of the hierarchy: fibre, yarn, strand, rope.** **Unevenness costs strength at all four, and it compounds.**
>
> **So the route to stronger rope is not more twist and not more hemp. It is MORE CONSISTENT YARN — which is a spinning problem, at SPIN-WHEEL-2, not a ropewalk problem.**

### ★★ The working-load rule, written down

| | |
|---|---|
| **Breaking load** | **Measured. **The only honest number, and it cost one length of rope |
| **WORKING LOAD = BREAKING ÷ 6** | Rope ages, chafes, wets, and I will not re-test it every time |
| ⚠ ★★ **A KNOT COSTS ANOTHER THIRD OR MORE** | **A rope breaks AT THE KNOT** — the fibres bend sharply and the outer ones take everything. **A SPLICED eye is far stronger than a knotted one.** *Learn to splice properly before anything hangs off a knot* |
| ⚠ ★★ **SHOCK LOADING MULTIPLIES ENORMOUSLY** | **A weight CAUGHT by a rope applies many times what it applies HANGING.** So **a static rating is meaningless for a fall.** **Never rate a climbing line by what it holds standing still** |

> **ROPE-2 IS CERTIFIED FOR LOADED HAUL AND LASHING, at the ÷6 figure, SPLICED not knotted.**
>
> **It is NOT yet certified for me hanging on it over a drop.** For that I want consistent yarn and a proper margin, and I would rather take the extra week than test it the other way.

Cheese and ricotta went together this time and the ricotta off fresh whey gave half again what seven litres of stale whey managed, which settles that argument for good. Three fixes to the walk. The traveller got a slot a third of the laid length and used about seven metres of its ten. The strands got an equal stone hung on each before hooking up, because I had caught myself planning to pull them all "about the same," and three identical stones are a cheaper instrument than a good eye and they do not get tired. And the third one is the one I am pleased with: the whirler is cranked, so the twist is already countable, and if I count the turns and measure the finished length then the lay stops being the words "hard" or "soft" and becomes a number I can write down and reproduce next year. I can specify a rope now rather than describe one. Thirty metres laid came off at twenty-three, so the shortening is about a quarter and now measured rather than guessed, and the lay is even the whole way — I rolled it under my foot end to end and there is no hard patch. Then I laid a spare length purely to destroy, because there is no way to learn what a rope carries except to break one, and rigged it over the gantry against known weights. It broke lower than I expected. Part of that is simply what rope is: the fibres lie at an angle, because the twist that holds them together is the same twist that stops any of them pulling straight along the load, and they are short and held only by friction on their neighbours, so a rope never realises the sum of its fibres. But part of it is mine. My hand-spun yarn is uneven, and a rope breaks at the thinnest place in the weakest yarn — which is the same argument as "only as strong as its most-loaded strand," one level further down, and it is true at all four levels and it compounds. So the road to stronger rope is not more twist and not more hemp; it is more consistent yarn, and that is a spinning problem, not a ropewalk problem. I have written the working load at a sixth of breaking, because rope ages and chafes and gets wet and I will not be re-testing it each time. A knot costs another third on top, because the rope breaks at the knot where the fibres turn sharply and the outer ones take everything, so I need to learn to splice properly before I hang anything off a knot. And a weight caught by a rope applies many times what the same weight applies hanging still, which means a static rating tells me nothing at all about a fall. Rope 2 is certified for loaded haul and lashing. It is not certified for me over a drop, and I would rather spend another week on the yarn than find out the other way.*
