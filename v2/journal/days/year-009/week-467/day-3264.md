## ★ Kisecik is the right rock and I already knew it without noticing

**The Kisecik veins are in OPHIOLITE.** I have been going up there for sulfur since d1611 and for zinc and lead since d1651 and writing the word down every time without hearing it.

> ★ **An ophiolite is a slice of ocean floor shoved up onto land, and it comes as a SEQUENCE: serpentinite at the base, then GABBRO, then a sheeted dyke swarm, then BASALT pillow lavas on top.** **Two of those four are exactly what a surface plate wants, and I have had a road to them for sixteen hundred days.**

## ★★ WHICH ROCK — and the answer is about how fast it COOLED

I went expecting to take whatever was hardest. **Hardness is not the question.**

> ★★ **A LAPPED SURFACE CAN NEVER BE FINER THAN THE CRYSTALS IT IS MADE OF.** Under the abrasive, an individual crystal does not wear — **it PLUCKS OUT WHOLE**, and leaves a pit the size of itself. **A coarse rock cannot hold a fine surface at any price.**

**Which turns the whole search into a geology question with one variable:**

> ★★ **GRAIN SIZE IS SET BY COOLING RATE. Slow cooling grows big crystals; fast cooling has no time to grow any.** **So I am not looking for a rock — I am looking for WHERE THE ROCK COOLED FASTEST.**

| Where | What it is | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Gabbro body** | Cooled slowly, deep | ☠ **Crystals visible to the naked eye. Useless — it will pluck** |
| **Middle of a dyke** | Medium | Marginal |
| ★★ **DYKE MARGINS and PILLOW RIMS** | **Chilled against cold rock or cold water — froze before anything could grow** | ★★ **THE PRIZE. Dense, dark, crystals I cannot resolve at all** |

**Took from the chilled margins of the sheeted dykes. Black, heavy, and when I broke a piece the fracture was almost glassy.**

### ⚠ Two ways the block can be bad in ways I cannot see

**VESICLES.** *Lava carries gas, and gas makes bubbles.* ☠ **A vesicular basalt is worthless as a reference — every bubble is a hole in the surface, and I will not find them until I have lapped for a week.** *Dyke rock is better than flow rock for exactly this reason: it froze under pressure with nowhere to bubble.*

**HIDDEN CRACKS**, from the quarry, from frost, from the original cooling.

> ★★ **AND BOTH ARE CAUGHT THE SAME FREE WAY: RING IT.** Rest the block on a soft point and strike it with a hammer. ★★ **Sound dense stone RINGS and holds the note. Vesicular or cracked stone THUDS and dies.** **An internal inspection for nothing but a tap — the cheapest measurement I have found since the hive heft.**

★ **And take from a FRESH BREAK, never a surface boulder** — *the outer few fingers of any exposed rock is a weathering rind: altered, soft, and it looks exactly like the rest.*

## ☠★★ AND THEN THE THING THAT CHANGES THE PLAN: A FRESH STONE MOVES

Sat down with a block in the yard and nearly started lapping. **Stopped, because of something I would only have discovered a year from now.**

> ☠ ★★ **A FRESHLY QUARRIED STONE IS UNDER STRESS AND IT RELAXES SLOWLY.** It has been held in compression inside a mountain for an unimaginable time; **I have just cut it free, and it will go on creeping into its new shape for months.**
>
> ★★ **If I lap it flat this week I will own a plate that is flat this week.** *And it will be a reference. And it will be wrong, and it will be wrong SILENTLY, and everything I build off it inherits the error.*

**So the project phases itself, and this is not a delay — it is the schedule:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **NOW** | **Rough the three plates out. Trim, square, flatten coarsely.** *Take the bulk of the stone off, because bulk removal is what releases the stress* |
| **THROUGH THE SUMMER** | ★ **LET THEM SIT. Outdoors, on three points, turned over now and then.** *They will move. That is the point — I want them to do their moving before they mean anything* |
| **AUTUMN** | ★★ **Then the three-plate lapping, fine and slow, on stone that has finished arguing** |

> ★★ **This is the same lesson as the tan pit and the jerky rack wearing a third face: HURRY SEALS THE SURFACE AND STRANDS THE MIDDLE.** *Here the middle is the stress, and rushing it strands the error inside a thing I will be trusting for a thousand years.*
>
> ★ **And it costs me nothing. The precision chain was never going to finish this summer, and summer is hemp and harvest anyway. The plates season while I do other work — the most patient possible use of a season.**

## Evening — grading the abrasive

**Crushed quartz, and then the part that makes it usable.**

> ★★ **GRADE BY SETTLING. Stir the crushed grit into a tall jar of water and wait a counted time; the coarse falls fast, the fine falls slow. Pour off the cloudy water into the next jar and wait longer. Repeat.** Each decanting is a grade, and **the times are arbitrary as long as they are the SAME every batch — because what I need is not a known grit size, it is a REPEATABLE ONE.**

★★ **Reproducible beats true, again — the Daniell cell's lesson, in a jar of muddy water.**

★ **And it is the perfect end-of-haul-day job: the work is WAITING.** *Stir, sit down, eat, pour, stir, wait longer.* **Four grades off the first run, coarse to very fine, each in its own labelled crock.**

⚠ *Wash each grade properly. **One coarse grain left in a fine grade will scratch a surface all the way through the finish step** — and I will not know which step betrayed me.*
