## ⚠ First, the idea from last night, taken seriously

**I did think about it. Walk over naked, dig, and find out about the gas the direct way — lose a spade, keep the knowledge.**

★★ **And I want to be honest that this is a real instrument and not a joke. I can perform destructive tests on myself. Nobody has ever been able to do that. It deserves a doctrine rather than a flinch.**

**But it is the wrong tool for THIS question, and the reason is precise:**

> ☠ ★★★ **GAS IN A PIT IS NOT A CONSTANT. It rises and falls with the pressure, the temperature, the wind and the hour** — *a hole that is harmless on a breezy afternoon can be lethal at dawn under high pressure.*
>
> ★★★ **SO THE TEST IS ASYMMETRIC: IT CAN CONFIRM DANGER AND IT CAN NEVER CLEAR IT.** **If I die, I learn "there was gas, that once" — which I already have to assume. If I live, I learn nothing at all, because a clean reading on a windy day says nothing about a still one.** ⚠ **A test that cannot return a useful negative is not a test, it is a ritual.**

★★ **And the answer I would have bought with it is already mine for nothing: A BELLOWS AND A PIPE. Ventilate and the question does not arise.** ★ *Plus the trench, which cannot pool gas at all.*

### ☠ Two things I had not thought about, and one of them is genuinely unpleasant

> ☠ ★★★ **WHAT HAPPENS TO THE OLD BODY?** *The rules say I respawn with a restored body and that my possessions stay where they were. **They do not say the corpse goes anywhere.*** ⚠ **If it stays, then a death-test at the bottom of a pit leaves my own body at the bottom of a pit I still cannot safely enter** — **and every future test litters this valley with me.** ★ *I have never died here. I do not actually know the answer, and I would rather find out somewhere retrievable than down a gas hole.*

⚠ **And the pain is real even where the damage is not.** *"Whatever consequences your species normally experiences" is not a small print I get to skip.*

### ★★ DEATH-TEST DOCTRINE — filed, because this will come up again

| A death-test is justified when | |
|---|---|
| ★★ **The information is otherwise UNOBTAINABLE** | *Not merely inconvenient. If a bellows answers it, a bellows answers it* |
| ★★ **A single sample is REPRESENTATIVE** | ☠ **Never for a quantity that varies with weather or hour** |
| ★★ **The cost is GEAR ONLY** | **Go naked, go empty-handed, and go somewhere I can walk back from** |
| ★ **Somewhere RETRIEVABLE** | **Never down a hole, never underwater, never far out** |
| ★ **The answer changes what I DO** | *Curiosity is not a reason to die* |

★ **Filed. Not used today.**

## ★★★ THE CAMPUS PLAN — and the first thing the survey did was contradict me

**Took the water level and a line round the whole campus envelope before drawing anything. ~1,400 m² if it is all eventually hard surface, bed, pen or building, against ~300 m² of hard surface now.**

### ☠ The survey's finding

> ☠ ★★★ **THREE OF MY OWN TERRACE BENCHES FALL BACKWARD, INTO THE CUT.** *I built the terraces to stop erosion, and a terrace is a FLAT BENCH WITH A CUT FACE BEHIND IT — **so every one of them is a trough with a wall at the uphill end.** Water arrives off the slope above, reaches the bench, and has nowhere to go but along the foot of the cut.*
>
> ★★ **I could not see this by eye. A bench that falls back by a finger over four paces LOOKS level, and it is the difference between a dry footing and a wet one for ninety days a year.**

★★★ **Which produces the rule I will use on every surface I ever lay here:**

> ★★★ **A "FLAT" SURFACE MUST NEVER ACTUALLY BE FLAT.** **Every bench, every slab, every yard gets a deliberate cross-fall — about a finger in five paces — toward somewhere I have CHOSEN.** ★★ **Level is for instruments. Everything outdoors gets a fall.**

★ **Fix: a back-drain along the foot of each cut face, and re-grade the bench to fall to it. Not big work. It has been quietly wetting three footings for years.**

## ★★★ THE ACTUAL PLANNING INSIGHT — drainage is the skeleton

**I sat down meaning to lay out beds and buildings. That is the wrong thing to fix first.**

> ★★★ **I DO NOT KNOW WHAT I WILL WANT IN TEN YEARS, AND I HAVE NEVER ONCE HAD A USE I DID NOT OUTGROW.** *A plot committed today is a plot I will regret.* ★★ **But the LEVELS and the WATER are different: they are what everything else sits on, and they are ruinous to change afterwards.**
>
> ★★★ **SO THE MASTER PLAN FIXES THE WATER AND THE CIRCULATION PERMANENTLY AND LEAVES THE PLOTS LOOSE.** *Beds, pens and yards can be re-drawn every year at no cost. A drain cannot.*

### ☠ ★★★ And the thing that would have caught me in five years

> ☠ ★★★ **EVERY HARD SURFACE I ADD SHEDS WATER THAT USED TO SOAK IN.** *Pavement does not make water disappear; it moves it, faster, to somewhere else.* **Going from ~300 m² to ~1,400 m² of hard surface is roughly FIVE TIMES the runoff arriving at the bottom of this campus.**
>
> ☠ ★★★ **AND YOU CANNOT RETROFIT CAPACITY INTO A DRAIN NETWORK. You can always add more AT THE TOP — another slab, another yard — but the water arrives at the BOTTOM, and the bottom is the part that is already built, already buried, and already the wrong size.**
>
> ★★★ **SIZE THE MAINS FOR THE FINAL PAVED AREA, NOW, WHILE THEY ARE STILL A TRENCH AND NOT A COMMITMENT.** *Paving piecemeal without doing this means chasing floods for a decade and never catching up.*

★★ **Corollary I like: keep some of the campus PERMEABLE on purpose.** *Beds, gravel, grass — every square metre I do not pave is a square metre the drains never have to carry.* ★★★ **The gardens are part of the drainage system, which means the flowers and the drainage were never two projects.**

## ★★ EARTHQUAKE IN A MASTER PLAN IS NOT ABOUT BUILDINGS

**The building code already handles walls. A LAYOUT can buy things a code cannot, and they are free at planning time and impossible later.**

| | |
|---|---|
| ★★★ **REDUNDANCY OF ACCESS** | **After a shake the question is not "what fell" — it is "can I still reach everything."** ⚠ **Never let the only path to the water, the stores or the gate run between two tall things** · **two ways to everywhere that matters** |
| ☠ ★★★ **DO NOT PUT THE SEED ARK, THE MUSEUM AND THE RECORDS IN ONE BUILDING** | **Distribution is the cheapest earthquake insurance there is. It costs NOTHING at planning time and it cannot be bought afterwards at any price** |
| ★★ **Nothing heavy uphill of anything essential** | *A wall that falls downhill falls onto whatever I put below it* |
| ★★ **Keep an open muster space** | **Somewhere with nothing tall around it — for working, for rebuilding, and for standing in** |
| ★ **Rebuild ROUTES, not just structures** | **The cart has to be able to get to the rubble** |

## Laid stone — but only where the plan is already proven

> ★★ **A LAYOUT TEACHES YOU THINGS BY BEING WALKED THAT IT CANNOT TEACH YOU BY BEING DRAWN** — *where I actually walk, where water actually stands, where the shade falls at which hour.* ★★★ **Stake it, live in it a season, THEN commit stone.** *Same discipline as the basalt datum: let it settle and read it rather than committing early.*

★ **So: stone today only on the beds along paths I have already worn bare, and on the two terrace benches I have worked for years. Everything else staked and left.**

### Border decisions

| | |
|---|---|
| ☠ ★★ **STONE at grade, never BRICK** | **A bed border stands in wet soil permanently. Fired brick wicks, and I have just spent a month learning what a wicking wall does to anything above it.** ★ **Brick lives ABOVE a damp course; stone lives in the ground** |
| ★★★ **DRY-LAID, not mortared** | **A mortared border cracks in frost and in a shake and then it is broken. A dry-laid one shuffles and is still a border.** ★★ *The right seismic response for a NON-structural element is to let it move* |
| ★★ **Lay this single course LEVEL AND TO A LINE anyway** | **So it can become the FIRST COURSE of a raised bed later.** *Costs nothing today, saves relaying all of it* |

**~18 m of border down. Four beds bounded. The rest staked.**

★ **And the beds go to bee forage first** — *sown for bloom into the June–August dearth, which is the gap that actually limits the apiary.*
