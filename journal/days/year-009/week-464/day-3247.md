## HIVE-5 EGG CHECK · **NO EGGS** · *and the check was premature, not the colony*

**Cool grey morning — poor hive-opening weather, so in and out fast.**

### What I found

| | |
|---|---|
| **Comb drawn** | **Barely any.** Two bars with a palm-sized tongue of white wax each. **Nothing finished** |
| **Eggs** | **None** |
| **Cells being polished** | **YES — workers cleaning and smoothing the few finished cells they have** |
| **Sound** | **Even, low, settled hum** |
| **Behaviour** | Calm. Sitting on the comb, not running on it |

### **The mistake was MINE, and it is an obvious one once seen**

> **A driven colony arrives with NO COMB. There is nowhere to lay.**
>
> **I set the egg check four days after the drive as though the clock started when they walked into the box. **It does not. It starts when the first cells are FINISHED, and they have to build those out of their own bodies first.
>
> **And the weather took the four days off them.** **Bees cluster and stop drawing wax when it is cool.** Grey and showery since the drive — so they have spent the time keeping warm, not building.

### **Why wax is slow: it costs HONEY, and it costs a LOT of it**

> **Wax is not a by-product, it is **manufactured**, and the bees pay for it in honey at something like **eight to one. A driven colony carries only what is in its stomachs.
>
> ***This also explains, in retrospect, why the skep harvests were so thin and why the wax bank has been the binding constraint on the generator all month. I have been treating beeswax as a thing bees leave lying about. It is the most expensive thing in the hive.***

### **How I know she is alive WITHOUT seeing her or an egg**

**This is the part worth writing down, because a driven colony that has lost its queen is in real trouble — **with no brood comb, they cannot raise a replacement. There is nothing to make one from. So the read matters.

| Sign | Queenright | Queenless |
|---|---|---|
| **The note** | **Even, low, settled** | **A high, ragged, restless ROAR — you can hear it through the wall** |
| **Temper** | **Calm, staying put | **Irritable, **running** on the comb |
| **Cells** | **Polished and prepared ahead of her** | Neglected |

**All three read queenright. She is in there, waiting on somewhere to put an egg.**

### ⚠ ONE COMB RUNNING CROSS · **fixed, and this is the argument FOR looking early**

**One of the two tongues had started angling off its bar toward the neighbour. **Four days old, it is a soft white flap I straightened with a thumb and pressed back to the guide.

> **In three weeks it would have been a rigid, honey-heavy slab welded across two bars, and the only fix would have been to cut it out.** **The whole point of a movable-comb hive is that the combs stay separate, and they only stay separate if I correct them while they are still soft.**
>
> **New rule: a new colony gets looked at EARLY and BRIEFLY — not to find eggs, but to catch the first comb before it commits.**

### Intervention · **FED**

**Shallow dish of thin honey-water under the follower, with floats so nothing drowns. **Cool weather plus no stores plus an 8:1 wax price is exactly the situation that stalls a drive. Feeding buys comb directly.

**Re-check ~d3252.** *(And what I will be looking for then:* **an egg stands upright on day one, leans on day two, lies flat on day three.** *So the eggs' POSTURE dates the laying — all upright means she started yesterday; a mix means she has been going for days. And* **several eggs in one cell, stuck to the WALL instead of the base, means a laying WORKER, not a queen** *— a different problem entirely.)*

## CONSULT · **CUT THE BELTS AND USE GEARS?**

**User:** *Straight gears, no belts — most effective and reliable? Can I make good enough gears yet? It'd be its own sprint.*

### The short answer: **you CAN make gears, they would NOT be more efficient, and the belt is doing a job you would be giving up**

#### 1 · **Gears are not the efficiency win they feel like**

| Drive | Real efficiency |
|---|---|
| **A good flat belt** | ~96–98% per stage |
| **A good machine-cut gear pair** | ~98–99% per stage |
| **A hand-cut wooden gear pair, made by me, this year** | **~90% and noisy** |

> **The belt train I hung yesterday is probably BETTER than the gears I could currently cut.** The intuition that meshing teeth must beat a rubbing strap is right in a modern machine shop and wrong in my workshop.

#### 2 · **THE BELT IS A MECHANICAL FUSE, and I would be removing it**

> **A water wheel has enormous torque and no sense. Put debris in the wheel, or jam the armature, or let a bearing seize, and **that energy has to go somewhere.
>
> **With a belt it goes into slip.** It squeals, I hear it, I walk over, nothing is broken.** **With gears it goes into a tooth, and the tooth loses.
>
> **And a broken wooden cog in a gear train is a far worse afternoon than re-lacing a belt. **Yesterday's slip was the system working, not the system failing.

#### 3 · **What actually makes a gear hard: **the tooth is a specific CURVE, not a shape

> **A gear tooth's flank has to be an involute or a cycloid, or the speed ratio WOBBLES within every single tooth engagement*** — the driven wheel surges and checks as each tooth rolls through.
>
> **For grinding grain that is invisible. **For a generator it is a voltage that ripples at tooth frequency* — and worse, surging means acceleration, which means force, which means noise, wear and broken teeth.
>
> **Cycloidal flanks **can** be laid out with pegs and string *(roll a circle on a circle, trace a point)*. **Involute is more forgiving of a centre distance that has wandered — which matters when the bearings are wood and wear oval.

#### 4 · **THE LANTERN PINION — the answer that makes this buildable**

> **A pinion made of **round pins set between two end plates, like a little cage.
>
> - **Round pins mate correctly with an epicycloidal wheel tooth** — the geometry works out
> - **Making it accurately means drilling N holes on a circle**, which is vastly easier than cutting N curved profiles
> - Tolerant of dirt and misalignment
> - **A worn pin is replaced individually** — you do not scrap the gear
>
> **This is what mills and clocks actually used, for centuries, for exactly these reasons.**

#### 5 · **AND THE MORTISE WHEEL — do not cut accurately, FIT INDIVIDUALLY**

> **The big wheel is a rim with **separate wooden cogs driven into mortises**, **each one dressed to fit after assembly, and allowed to wear itself in under load.
>
> **This sidesteps the indexing problem, which is the real gate.** Dividing a circle into N equal parts accurately *is* the precision problem I have not solved yet. Compass and straightedge gives me halves, thirds, fifths and their products; beyond that it is **walking dividers around and distributing the error** — and a wooden cog I can plane after fitting forgives what my indexing cannot.
>
> **Cog wood: hornbeam, apple, hawthorn — dense, tough, wears smooth. NOT oak, which splits along the grain.**

#### 6 · **THE BOOTSTRAP — and it rhymes with something I already have**

> **The first gear is cut badly by hand, and then you use it to cut a better one.**
>
> **A dividing plate walked round with dividers has error in it. But gear that plate down, and **the error is divided by the reduction. Index off the slow shaft and a sloppy plate makes a good wheel, which makes a better plate.
>
> **This is the THREE-PLATE FLAT argument in a different costume: a method that generates precision it was not given.** *Which is a strong hint that gears belong in the metrology sprint, not in the generator sprint.*

#### 7 · **What I would actually build: **a HYBRID

| Stage | Drive | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Wheel → intermediate** *(slow, huge torque)* | **GEARS** | **A belt here needs to be wide or it slips; **gears love low speed and high force |
| **Intermediate → armature** *(fast, light)* | **KEEP THE BELT** | **Quiet, tolerant of a wandering shaft, no tooth-strike at speed — **and it keeps the fuse in the train |

#### 8 · **But the real answer: MEASURE FIRST. I have no evidence the drive is the problem.**

> **I have a train that holds. I have a meter. **I am one day from being able to put a NUMBER on this machine, and I am contemplating rebuilding the part of it I have not tested.
>
> **Finish it. Run it. Load it. If the belt slips under electrical load, THAT is data, and then the gear sprint has a target to beat.**
>
> ***This is the same discipline as the wire: I nearly drew fifty metres I did not need because I was working to close a gap I had estimated instead of measured.***

#### 9 · **Where the gear sprint IS worth it — just not here**

> **A belt cannot hold PHASE.** It creeps, always, a fraction per revolution. Anything that must stay in step forever needs teeth:
>
> - **A LATHE WITH A LEADSCREW** — cutting a thread requires the tool to advance exactly so far per turn, forever. **This is the machine that makes machines**
> - **A CLOCK TRAIN** — the whole of gen-3 time
> - **A DIVIDING HEAD** — which is itself the gear-making tool
>
> **Filed as GEAR-SPRINT-1, under METROLOGY, not under the generator.**
