## ★ GEN-WW-1 · **BUILD CLOSE**

### 1 · COMMUTATOR-2

**Built to the d3233 design. **Hardwood hub turned true, segments seated in the plaster jig, mica-thin gaps, ramps dressed at the ENDS only.

> **Design reconciliation:** the plan said **×4 coils × 95 turns** and the design said **odd segment count.** These fight — four coils give four segments. **Re-divided to ×5 coils × 76 turns — the same 380 turns of wire, and five segments.** *Nothing lost, and the refinement I wrote down four days ago actually gets used instead of quietly dropped.*

### 2 · **THE WINDING IS ONE CLOSED LOOP, NOT FIVE COILS**

**This is the thing I had wrong in my head until I laid it out.**

> **I had been picturing five separate coils, each with two ends, each wired to something. **It is not that.
>
> **The end of coil 1 joins the start of coil 2, and THAT JUNCTION is what goes to a commutator segment.** Round and round, coil 5 back into coil 1 — **the armature winding is a CLOSED RING, and the brushes simply TAP IT at two points.**
>
> **So at any instant the current has **two paths** from brush to brush, going opposite ways round the ring, in parallel. **The commutator's job is not to "switch the coil" — it is to keep the tap points standing still in space while the ring rotates underneath them.

**Wound all five the same handedness, start leads marked with a knot of linen thread. **One coil wound backwards subtracts instead of adds, and you cannot see it — you can only prevent it. Paper between layers. ~95 m of wire in, leads off the last nine.

### 3 · Field

**MAGNETO — ×4 MAG-BOOT-EM rods** in an iron yoke, poles faced to the armature. Permanent field, live from the first turn.

## ★ **TUNING THE BRUSHES · the adjustable gear earns itself in ten minutes**

**First run sparked at the brushes — a blue fizz all the way round.**

> **The brushes must sit where the coil they are shorting has NO voltage in it — the "neutral" position. Anywhere else and the commutator is breaking a live circuit five times a revolution, which is what the spark IS.**
>
> **Slackened the brush gear and walked it round a few degrees at a time, watching. **Spark fell, went to a faint edge, then went out. Locked it.
>
> **AND THEN — put a load on it and the spark CAME BACK.** The neutral position **MOVES UNDER LOAD** *(the armature's own current makes its own magnetism, and it drags the neutral round with it)*. **So I tuned it again, loaded, and left it there.**
>
> **You cannot set brush timing on a stopped machine, or even on an unloaded one. You set it running, at the load you mean to use.** *That is exactly why I gave it an adjustable brush gear, and I am very glad I did.*

*End Day 3248 · Cal-Y10 D103 · ~4 Apr. It runs. The needle moved and stayed moved, and then I spent the better part of the afternoon finding out exactly how well, which is a thing I have never once been able to do before. Two corrections to my own understanding came out of the build. The first is that an armature winding is not five coils with ten ends — it is a closed ring, each coil's tail joined to the next one's head with the junction going to a segment, so the current runs two ways round the loop in parallel and the brushes just tap it. The commutator is not switching coils on and off; it is holding the tap points still in space while the ring spins underneath. The second came from the sparking. Brushes have to sit where the coil they momentarily short has no voltage in it, or the machine is breaking a live circuit five times a turn, and that arcing is the circuit breaking. I walked the brush gear round until the spark died, felt pleased with myself, put a load on, and the spark came straight back — because the armature's own current makes its own magnetism and drags that neutral point round with it. You cannot time a commutator on a still machine, or even a spinning empty one. You do it running, under the load you actually intend. Then the measurement, and it came in well under my arithmetic, and I am oddly delighted about it. A year ago today would have ended with "it works" and I would have learned precisely nothing. Instead the shortfall broke apart into a ranked list: the wheel droops from twelve revolutions to eight the moment it is loaded, and I had built every calculation on the free-running figure, which is a third of the machine lost to a number I never checked. The air gap is too generous because I did not trust my own concentricity, and the gap — not the iron — is what governs a magnetic circuit, so closing it nearly doubles the field for the price of careful fitting and no materials at all. Brush drop and belt losses account for the rest. None of that is the design failing. Three of the four I could only have found by measuring, and the largest was my own mistake. And the thing to remember, while squinting at a small number on a scale: the pedal generator's output is limited by my legs, and this one was still turning at dusk with nobody watching it.*
