## GEN-WW-1 · ASSEMBLY DAY 1

### Frame + mount

**Oak frame bolted to the wheelhouse sill, clear of the splash. **Quick-release collar on WW-2's output so the generator can come off without touching the wheel.** Three shafts: **wheel → intermediate → armature.

### The gearing, as designed

| Stage | Ratio | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **WW-2 → intermediate** | **~4:1** | Big pulley on the slow shaft, small on the fast |
| **Intermediate → armature** | **~4:1** | *The intermediate shaft carries a BIG pulley and a SMALL pulley — that is what makes it a step-up and not a jackshaft* |
| **Overall** | **~16:1** | **~12 rpm at the wheel → ~190 rpm at the armature** |

> **Two stages, not one.** A single 16:1 needs a pulley small enough to be a dowel, **and a tiny pulley has almost no belt on it.**

## ⚠ FIRST SPIN-UP · **THE BELT SLIPPED** · *and this is the good kind of failure*

**Opened the gate. Wheel turned. Intermediate shaft turned. **The armature pulley squealed, crept, and stalled under no load at all.

### Diagnosis · **it is not tension. It is WRAP.**

> **A flat belt's grip is not set by how hard you pull it. It is set by HOW MUCH OF THE PULLEY THE BELT TOUCHES.**
>
> **On a 4:1 step-up the belt comes off a big pulley and onto a small one, so **it barely brushes the small one** — well under half its circumference. **The small pulley is always the one that slips, and the small pulley is always the one that matters.
>
> ***I had been tightening it, which just loaded the bearings and stretched the belt.***

### Three fixes, all cheap

| Fix | What it does |
|---|---|
| **IDLER PULLEY** | **A free-running roller pressed against the slack side **near the small pulley**, pushing the belt round further. **Buys wrap without buying tension |
| **SLACK SIDE ON TOP** | **A belt has a tight side and a slack side, and which one is up matters.** Put the **slack** side **uppermost** and it **sags into the pulleys under its own weight, adding wrap for free.** Slack side underneath sags *away* and takes wrap off |
| **CROWN THE PULLEYS** | See below |

### **CROWNING · the fact I would never have guessed**

> **A flat belt climbs toward the HIGHEST part of a pulley.**
>
> **So a pulley turned with a **slight barrel — fatter in the middle** — **self-centres the belt. It runs to the crown and stays there, and it corrects itself when it wanders.
>
> **Flanges do NOT work.** A flange does not stop the belt wandering, it just **stands in the way of a belt that is already wandering** — and then **chews the edge off it.** **The belt is the expensive part; do not build something that eats it.**

**Re-turned both driving pulleys with a crown of maybe a millimetre and a half over the face. Fitted an idler on the second stage. Re-hung the belt slack-side-up.**

**Second spin-up: **held. No squeal, no creep, belt tracking dead centre and staying there.

### Cost of the gearing, honestly

**Three shafts, six bearings, two belt stages. **Each belt stage costs a few percent, and the losses MULTIPLY rather than add.** Call it ~90% through the train. **I will pay that gladly — the alternative was a kilometre of copper.

## LAMINATED CORE · **cut and prepared**

**Drew iron bloom out to thin sheet, cut to the armature profile, **good yield.

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Laminations run ALONG the flux** | **The sheets lie **in the plane the magnetism travels, so the eddy-current loops — which want to run *across* — are the ones that get cut |
| **ANNEAL after cutting, not before** | **Cutting and hammering **hardens** the iron, and **hard iron keeps its magnetism** — that is hysteresis loss and residual magnetism, both of which I pay for on every single revolution.** **Soft iron forgets. I want it to forget** |
| **DEBURR EVERY SHEET** | **A burr bridges one lamination to the next and re-shorts the stack.** All that work undone by an edge I could not be bothered to stone. **Every sheet, both faces, stoned flat** |
| **Insulate between** | **Thin rosin varnish** — rosin dissolved in my own turpentine, brushed on, dried. *The turpentine still is already paying rent* |
| **Clamp the stack HARD** | **A loose lamination stack **buzzes — iron physically changes shape in a magnetic field, so a slack sheet vibrates against its neighbour |

**Stack clamped, varnished, set to dry overnight.**

*End Day 3246 · Cal-Y10 D101 · ~2 Apr. Showery, so a shed day, which suited it. The frame went on the sill and the two belt stages went up, and then the whole thing squealed and stalled under no load whatsoever, which was the most useful thing that happened all day. I had been thinking about a belt as a thing you tighten. It is not. Its grip comes from how much of the pulley it wraps around, and on a step-up the belt hardly touches the small pulley at all — which means the small pulley is always the one that slips, and it is always the one that matters. Tightening it harder had only been loading the bearings and stretching the leather. An idler roller pressed on the slack side buys the wrap back for nothing, and hanging the belt with its slack side uppermost lets it sag into the pulleys and add a little more for free. But the fact I will remember is about crowning: a flat belt climbs toward the highest part of a pulley, so a pulley made slightly fatter in the middle steers the belt to its own centre and keeps correcting it. I would have put flanges on and been proud of them, and a flange does not steer a belt at all, it just gets in the way of one already going wrong and shaves the edge off it. The core laminations are cut, annealed after shaping rather than before — hard iron holds its magnetism and I would be paying for that every revolution — deburred one sheet at a time, because a single burr bridges the stack and undoes the entire point of it, and varnished with rosin cut in my own turpentine. Clamped tight, because loose iron in a magnetic field buzzes. Tomorrow the commutator, and then I finally get to put a number on something I have built.*
