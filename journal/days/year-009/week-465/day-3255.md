## ★★ **WHY A ROPE HOLDS TOGETHER AT ALL** · *the thing I had never actually thought about*

> I have laid rope by hand for years and never once asked why it does not simply unwind.
>
> **Because every layer is twisted the OPPOSITE WAY from the one inside it.**
>
> - **Fibres spun **one way** into **yarn
> - **Yarns twisted **the other way** into **strands
> - **Strands twisted **back the first way** into **rope
>
> **Each layer is straining to untwist itself, and the layer around it is straining the other way. The rope is in permanent, balanced tension WITH ITSELF.**
>
> **That is not a manufacturing trick. That IS the rope.** Untwist it and there is nothing there but loose hair.

### **And the trade nobody tells you about**

| More twist | Less twist |
|---|---|
| **Tougher against **abrasion and water** | **Stronger in **straight pull** |
| **But SHORTER and WEAKER in tension** *(fibres lying at an angle do not pull in line)* | **But looser, and it **abrades and opens |

> **Hard lay for rigging that takes weather. Soft lay for a pull that has to be strong. There is no "best" — there is a choice, and I should make it deliberately each time.**

## ⚠ ROPE-1 · **UNEVEN LAY** · *the traveller ran out of travel*

**Laid ~24 m of three-strand hemp. **It came out lumpy — hard in the last third, softer before it.

### The cause

> ★★ **A ROPE SHORTENS AS IT CLOSES — by something like a QUARTER.**
>
> **The strands stop being straight lines and become helices, and a helix around a length needs **more fibre than the length. So the finished rope is markedly shorter than what you laid out.
>
> **I gave the traveller about a metre of run. It needed six.**
>
> **It ran to the end of its slot partway through, and from that point the rope could not shorten — so instead of closing at constant tension it **closed by dragging everything tighter and tighter. Hence a hard, over-laid last third.

### **Why that is not cosmetic**

> **A rope is only as strong as its most-loaded strand**, and an uneven lay guarantees the load is not shared. **The hard section will take the pull first and part first.**
>
> **ROPE-1 IS LASHING GRADE. It is NOT going on a cliff and it is NOT going under a load with me attached.**

### Corrections — **all cheap, all in the machine**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Traveller slot ~⅓ of the laid length** | Lay 30 m, give it 10 m of run |
| **Equalise all three strand tensions BEFORE closing** | Uneven in = uneven out |
| **Draw the top back at a STEADY rate** | The pace sets the lay |
| **Whip the ends the instant it comes off** | Or the whole thing unlays itself |

### ★ **And the discipline: **PROOF-TEST, DO NOT ASSUME

> **A rope that will carry me has to be PROVED first, not believed.**
>
> **Hang it over the gantry with a **known weight** — and **I can make a known weight now, because I have mass standards** — at **well more than what it will ever be asked to carry, and leave it hanging.
>
> **Never certify a rope on its first run off a new machine. Rope-2 gets proof-tested. Rope-1 lashes firewood.**

**The machine is right. The calibration is not, and calibration is a morning.**

*End Day 3255 · Cal-Y10 D110 · ~11 Apr. Built the rope walk, and in laying out the whirler I finally asked a question I should have asked years ago, which is why a rope does not simply unwind itself. It is because every layer is twisted the opposite way from the one inside it — fibres one way into yarn, yarns the other way into strands, strands back the first way into rope — so each layer is straining to untwist and the layer around it is straining against it. The whole thing sits in permanent balanced tension with itself. That is not a trick of the manufacture; that is what a rope is. Untwist it and you have loose hair. The machine came out well: a geared three-hook whirler so all the strands turn together, a grooved cone that rides between them and holds them apart so the lay forms at a single travelling point rather than raggedly along the whole length, a far hook free to run, and forty metres of pegged straight ground. Which is the other obvious thing — a rope walk is long because the rope is; you cannot make thirty metres of rope in a ten-metre shed, and that is why these were built as sheds a bowshot long. The rope itself was not good. Twenty-four metres of three-strand hemp, hard in the last third and softer before it, and the reason is that a rope shortens as it closes, by something like a quarter, because the strands stop being straight lines and become helices and a helix wants more fibre than the length it wraps. I gave the travelling hook about a metre of run. It needed six. It hit the end of its slot partway along and after that the rope could not shorten, so instead of closing at an even tension it closed by dragging everything tighter and tighter. That is not a cosmetic fault. A rope is only as strong as its most-loaded strand, and an uneven lay guarantees the load is not shared, so the hard section takes the pull first and parts first. Rope 1 lashes firewood. It does not go on a cliff and it does not go under a load with me hanging on the end of it. Rope 2 gets a longer slot, equal tension on all three before closing, a steady draw, and then it gets proof-tested with a known weight — which I can produce now, so the mass standards have paid out somewhere I never expected them to. The machine is right. The calibration is not, and calibration is a morning.*
